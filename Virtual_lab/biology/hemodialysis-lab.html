Zoomed membrane — what a pore actually does

Red blood cell — 7 µm, rebounds off the wall Urea — 0.36 nm, slips through the pore Dialysate ions — isotonic, stay balanced Pore — 2–3 nm

Nothing pushes the urea across. It moves only because the bath holds none of it — remove that difference and the crossing stops, even at full pump pressure.

Fact check: a normal kidney filters ~180 L/day → 1–2 L urine. Dialysis has no tubular reabsorption, so you lose that efficiency. That is why dialysis takes 4 hours, 3× a week.

Teacher view • instructions & outcomes

  • Learning outcomes: explain the artificial-kidney principle; separate diffusion from active transport; relate 180 L filtrate → 1–2 L urine against dialysis with no reabsorption.
  • Demo flow: start paused, ask “why does the yellow fluid carry no urea?”, then press Play and point at the brown molecules crossing in step 3.
  • Misconception to address: students read pump pressure as the thing that filters waste. Pore size excludes the cells, but urea moves on concentration, not pressure — the slider challenge proves it.
  • Assignment: the drag-drop checks textbook diagram labels; the slider probes the gradient concept.
  • Extension: if the patient drinks excess water, what happens to dialysate composition? (Adjust Na⁺ to hold isotonicity.)
  • Sound: the pump thump tracks the speed slider and each ping is one urea crossing. Mute before a long discussion.
