Combination Reaction: Quick Lime + Water

Section 1.2.1 · Activity 1.4 · Figure 1.3 — formation of slaked lime

In Class IX we learnt that during a chemical reaction, atoms of one element do not change into those of another — bonds between atoms break, new bonds are made, and new substances appear. When calcium oxide (quick lime) meets water it reacts vigorously: the two reactants combine into a single product, calcium hydroxide (slaked lime), releasing a large amount of heat. A reaction in which a single product is formed from two or more reactants is a combination reaction.

Combination reaction Exothermic — releases heat Quick lime → slaked lime
CaO(s)quick lime + H2O(l) ⟶ Ca(OH)2 (aq)slaked lime + Heat (1.13)

Fact check: the vigorous bubbling you see is not a gas being produced — there is no gas on the product side of equation 1.13. The reaction releases so much heat that the water itself boils: the bubbles are steam.

Teacher view

Learning outcomes

  • Define a combination reaction and identify CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ as one, with correct state symbols.
  • Classify the reaction as exothermic from observable evidence (warm beaker, boiling).
  • Connect the names quick lime (CaO) and slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂) to their formulae.
  • Explain the milky appearance as a suspension of slightly soluble Ca(OH)₂.

Suggested demo flow (10–12 min)

  • Run Step 1–2 at 0.5× speed with Labels on; ask students to predict what "touch the beaker" will reveal.
  • Pause at Step 3; open Show molecules and let students watch bonds form and neighbours speed up — that is the heat.
  • Run Step 4; ask "what are the bubbles?" before revealing the fact-check line.
  • Finish on the equation strip; have students copy it with state symbols.

The misconception to attack

Most students read the bubbling as a gas being produced. Push them to the equation: no gas appears on the product side. The bubbling is water boiling from the heat released. Question 3 of the assignment tests exactly this.

Assignment notes (13 pts)

  • Q1 (3) — labels the Figure 1.3 diagram they will be examined on.
  • Q2 (2) — the Activity 1.4 observation: warm beaker ⇒ exothermic.
  • Q3 (2) — the misconception, head-on.
  • Q4 (3) — all-or-nothing multi-select on the core facts.
  • Q5 (3) — parameter challenge; the ΔT-per-gram model (≈3.2 °C/g in 100 mL) is illustrative, not measured — verify against your own values before quoting numbers.

Extension question

Slaked lime is used to whitewash walls. What does the whitewash slowly react with in air, and why does the wall develop a shiny finish after a few days? (CO₂ → CaCO₃, back to the chemistry of lime.)

⚠ Lab safety

CaO + water is strongly exothermic and caustic — demonstrate, do not hand out. Wear eye protection, add water to the lime slowly (never lime to a little water), and never seal the vessel. The mixture and beaker get hot enough to scald.

Particle inspector — CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂

H₂O 18 · Ca²⁺ 0 · OH⁻ 0 · Ca(OH)₂ 0

Live collision physics, not a scripted loop: when a water molecule strikes a CaO unit hard enough, the lattice unit breaks apart, hydroxide ions form, and each new Ca(OH)₂ bond releases energy — watch the neighbouring particles speed up. That speed-up is the heat you feel through the glass.
